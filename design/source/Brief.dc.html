How honeybees choose a new home

Ten thousand bees, no leader, and a decision that is almost always right.

Every spring, a crowded honeybee colony does something remarkable: it splits. The old queen leaves with thousands of workers, and the swarm hangs from a branch in a quiet cluster while it decides where to live next.

The queen doesn’t decide. A few hundred older workers — the scouts — fly out to inspect hollow trees and crevices. A good home has a cavity of roughly forty litres, a small entrance, and some height off the ground.

A scout that finds a promising site returns and performs a waggle dance on the swarm. The angle of the dance points toward the site; its length encodes distance. The better the site, the longer she dances — and the more recruits go to see for themselves.

Rival sites are settled in a way that looks almost like debate. Scouts backing one site deliver a brief head-butt and a short vibration — a stop signal — to bees dancing for its rivals. Weaker options lose their advocates; stronger ones gather momentum.

No single bee compares every option. The swarm waits for a quorum: once twenty or thirty scouts are at the same site at once, they return and begin piping, a signal that tells the swarm to warm its flight muscles. Soon the whole cloud lifts off together.

The biologist Thomas Seeley, who spent decades watching this, argues it is a model for good group decisions: many options, open debate, independent checking, and a clear threshold for agreement.
